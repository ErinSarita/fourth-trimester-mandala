The Fourth Trimester Mandala

The first weeks after a baby is born are tender, tiring, and tremendous. This mandala is a companion for them. It is written from the pre- and perinatal point of view, which holds that a baby is aware, feeling, and remembering from the very beginning, and that how she is met in these weeks matters. It gathers what research and experience tell us about the first twelve weeks, so a family and the people around them can meet this time with understanding. However the birth unfolded, these weeks are a time to rest, to heal, and to come to know each other.

About this mandala

A mandala is a circle drawn around a center, used in many traditions to see the bigger picture: how many parts belong together as one whole. This one looks at the fourth trimester, the twelve or so weeks after birth. At its center are a mother and her baby, because in these weeks they are best understood as one. It continues where The Pregnancy and Birth Mandala ends.

The lens

Pre- and perinatal education looks at pregnancy, birth, and the first year through the baby's eyes as well as the parents'. It starts from the understanding that a baby is a feeling, remembering person from before birth. She takes in how she is held, spoken to, and answered, and she tells us about it through her crying and her body. It also sees the mother as someone who needs to be seen and cared for. When a mother is held, she can hold her baby, and a baby who feels understood can settle and begin to trust.

Why a fourth trimester

A human baby is born very immature. Nils Bergman notes that her brain is only about a quarter developed at birth, and the first weeks are a critical window for its wiring. Human milk is low in protein and fat, the milk of a species whose young are carried and fed often, not left in a nest or a hiding place between feeds. In this sense the gestation continues after birth, on the mother's body instead of inside it. As Bergman puts it, the mother's body is the newborn's habitat, and breastfeeding, sleep, and connection are her niche: what that habitat makes possible.

What it is for

The message of this mandala is simple. These weeks are not about getting back to normal. They are about being close, being cared for, and coming to know each other, with support around you. Babies and mothers do best when they are kept together, fed and answered, and surrounded by people who are supported too. The mandala puts that into practice: what helps, why it helps, and who can help you do it.

Protecting this window

If you are expecting, it helps to think of the fourth trimester as a window worth protecting. Many families have about twelve weeks at home, and that is a good start. Paid leave of twelve weeks or more is linked with longer breastfeeding, warmer interaction between mother and baby, and healthier babies, and longer leave goes further (Nandi et al., 2018). If you can plan for more, through the first year or beyond, with both parents present as much as possible, so much the better. If you cannot, what matters most is that your baby is with a steady, responsive caregiver she knows well, whether that is her mother, her father, a grandparent, or another trusted person.

Support in layers

Ray Castellino taught that everyone who gives support needs support too, and that a mother and baby each need at least two layers of it (Castellino, in White, 2013). Here the first layer is the partner: one solid, consistent person around the mother. Families take many shapes, so that person may be the baby's father, another parent, a grandmother, or a close friend. What matters is that there is at least one, and that someone holds them too.

How to use it

Think of it as a tool, not a program. It does not ask you to do everything, or to do it perfectly. Take what is useful, and leave the rest.

Families often plan carefully for the birth and much less for what comes after, and many live far from their own parents. Parents can use this mandala to plan for the weeks after birth, and to see who might help with what. Educators can use it to teach, a quarter at a time: the foundations and the first hour in the third trimester, around weeks 28 to 34, alongside the birth quarter of The Pregnancy and Birth Mandala, and the first days and months in the last weeks of pregnancy, and again as each arrives.

At any point, you can open a slice to see, in one place, what is happening for the baby, the mother, the partner, the circle, and the educator, along with practices, and a way back if something went differently than hoped.

When it was hard

Stress becomes trauma when it overwhelms the ability to cope, and Matthew Appleton argues that what turns a hard birth into lasting trauma for a baby is less the birth itself than whether anyone understood what she went through. Babies tell their stories through crying and through their bodies. When the adults around them listen, name what happened, and stay, babies can release it and become relaxed and resilient (Appleton, 2017). The same is true for the mother: being heard is part of healing (Kendall-Tackett). So each slice ends with a repair: a way back, with words to say. It is not too late to begin.

A note on language

The baby is called she, following the Pregnancy and Birth Mandala. The partner is the first layer of support, whoever fills that role, and mother means the person who gave birth and is caring for the baby. Breastfeeding is described in depth because much of the research on these weeks centers on it. When breastfeeding is not possible, or not chosen, the habitat stays the same: skin to skin, feeding close and on cue, and being held. Every family and every baby is its own, and this is an educational map, not medical advice. A mother or baby who seems unwell needs prompt care from their provider.
